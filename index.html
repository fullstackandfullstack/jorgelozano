About me

A developer who reads the code before rewriting it

I started out the way a lot of people my age did: a cheap laptop, a folder of HTML files and far too much patience for things that refused to work. That curiosity turned into a career. Today I work as a senior fullstack engineer, which in practice means I am the person a team calls when a feature touches five layers at once and nobody is quite sure where the truth lives.

Most of my work happens in TypeScript. On the server that usually means Node with a properly modelled PostgreSQL database underneath; on the client it means React, careful state boundaries and markup that still makes sense with the stylesheet switched off. I am comfortable in Python and PHP when a project already lives there, and I have never believed that a stack is a personality.

What I actually care about is legibility. Software is read far more often than it is written, and the cost of a confusing module is paid every single sprint by whoever inherits it. So I write boring code on purpose: small functions, explicit names, honest error handling, tests around the parts that would genuinely hurt if they broke. I would rather ship something obvious on Tuesday than something clever that needs a meeting to explain.

I live and work in Barcelona, in the Sant Gervasi neighbourhood, and I collaborate with teams across Europe and North America. Remote work suits me — I keep predictable hours, I write things down, and I answer questions before I am asked them twice.

How a project runs

No surprises, from first call to handover

Most projects fail on communication rather than code. This is the sequence I follow, and I am happy to adapt it to whatever process a team already trusts.

  1. Understand the problem

    A conversation about what the business actually needs, who the users are, what already exists and what genuinely cannot change. I ask uncomfortable questions early because they are much cheaper now than in week six.

  2. Shape and estimate

    A short written plan: architecture sketch, the risky parts named out loud, a scope we can ship in slices, and time estimates given as ranges with the assumptions attached.

  3. Build in the open

    Small pull requests, a deployed environment from the first week, and a weekly demo of something real. Priorities can change between slices without derailing the whole plan.

  4. Harden and hand over

    Tests where they earn their keep, monitoring that pages the right person, documentation written for whoever comes next, and a walkthrough session so the team owns the code rather than renting it.
